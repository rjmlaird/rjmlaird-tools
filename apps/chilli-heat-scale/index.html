    Assumptions and cautions
    • This is an approximation. It assumes the pepper’s representative (median) SHU is evenly distributed through the final sauce.
    • Actual heat varies substantially between individual peppers and growing conditions.
    • Seeds and placental tissue (the pale ribs) can hold disproportionately high capsaicinoid concentrations.
    • Cooking does not reliably remove capsaicin. Dilution, fat and serving size all change perceived heat.
    • Start low, taste cautiously and add heat a little at a time.

    How to read this scale

    The Scoville scale was devised by Wilbur Scoville in 1912, using dilution until a taste panel could no longer detect heat. Today, heat is normally measured by chemical analysis (high-performance liquid chromatography) of capsaicinoid content, then converted to SHU. It quantifies capsaicinoid concentration. It does not directly measure how hot a person will find a pepper, which depends on the person, the dish and what is eaten with it.

    Ranges are shown because individual peppers differ. The representative value used for slider positions, comparisons and the calculator is the midpoint of each range.

    Safety for peppers above 100,000 SHU

    • Avoid touching your eyes, nose and mouth, and wash hands, knives and boards thoroughly.
    • Consider gloves when preparing large quantities, and ventilate the kitchen when cooking very hot peppers.
    • Label very hot dishes clearly, and keep them away from children and anyone who has not agreed to eat them.
    • Offer dairy-based accompaniments such as yoghurt or milk, which help more than water.
